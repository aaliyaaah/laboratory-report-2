# Experiment 9 – Frequency Modulation (FM)

---

# Overview

This experiment introduces the concept of **frequency modulation (FM)** and demonstrates how a message signal affects the **frequency of a carrier wave**. Unlike amplitude modulation (AM), FM encodes information by varying the **frequency** of the carrier rather than its amplitude.

FM is widely used in communication systems because it is **less susceptible to noise** compared to amplitude-based modulation schemes.

This laboratory experiment demonstrates:

- Generation of a frequency modulated signal  
- FM using squarewave and speech signals  
- Observation of FM spectral composition  

---

# Theory

## Frequency Modulation

Frequency modulation is a type of analog modulation where the **frequency of the carrier signal varies according to the amplitude of the message signal**, while the carrier amplitude remains constant.

An FM signal can be represented mathematically as:

s(t) = Ac cos(2πfct + β sin(2πfmt))

Where:

Ac = Carrier amplitude  
fc = Carrier frequency  
fm = Message signal frequency  
β = Modulation index  

Important characteristics of FM signals:

- The **carrier amplitude remains constant**
- The **carrier frequency changes based on the message signal**
- FM signals are **more resistant to noise**

---

# Conceptual Signal Behavior

A **message signal** (squarewave or speech) is applied to the modulator.

A **carrier signal** is generated by the Voltage Controlled Oscillator (VCO).

The resulting **FM signal** changes frequency depending on the amplitude of the message signal. When the message voltage increases, the carrier frequency increases. When the message voltage decreases, the carrier frequency decreases.

---

# Experiment Objectives

1. Generate a **frequency modulated signal using a squarewave**
2. Generate an **FM signal using speech**
3. Observe the **spectral composition of FM signals**

---

# Part A – Frequency Modulating a Squarewave

In this part of the experiment, a **squarewave message signal** is used to modulate a carrier produced by the **VCO module**.

## Procedure Summary

1. Configure the oscilloscope **timebase to 20 µs/div**  
2. Adjust the **VCO frequency control** to observe the unmodulated carrier waveform  
3. Apply the **squarewave message signal** to the modulator input  
4. Observe the resulting **frequency modulated waveform** on the oscilloscope  

## Observation

When the message signal changes polarity:

- The **carrier frequency increases** when the message is positive  
- The **carrier frequency decreases** when the message is negative  

However, the **carrier amplitude remains constant**.

---

# Why Does the Carrier Frequency Change?

**Question 1**

Why does the carrier frequency change when the message signal is applied?

**Answer**

The carrier frequency changes because the **Voltage Controlled Oscillator (VCO)** adjusts its output frequency according to the **input control voltage**. When the message signal is applied to the VCO input, the voltage level varies with time.

As the input voltage increases, the VCO increases its oscillation frequency. When the input voltage decreases, the oscillation frequency decreases. This causes the instantaneous frequency of the carrier signal to vary in proportion to the message signal.

Therefore, the VCO converts the **amplitude variations of the message signal into corresponding frequency variations**, which is the fundamental principle of frequency modulation.

---

# Part B – Generating an FM Signal Using Speech

Instead of a squarewave, the **speech module** is used as the message signal.

## Procedure Summary

1. Disconnect the squarewave source  
2. Connect the **speech module output** to the modulator input  
3. Set the oscilloscope trigger to **CH2**  
4. Speak or hum into the microphone  
5. Observe the FM waveform on the oscilloscope  

## Observation

The frequency of the carrier varies continuously depending on the **speech amplitude and pitch**.

Higher amplitude voice signals produce **larger frequency deviation**.

---

# Relationship Between Frequency Deviation and Message Amplitude

**Question 2**

What is the relationship between the FM signal's frequency deviation and the amplitude of the message?

**Answer**

The **frequency deviation increases as the amplitude of the message signal increases**.

This means that stronger input signals cause the carrier frequency to deviate further from its center frequency.

---

# Relationship Between Frequency Deviation and Message Frequency

**Question 3**

What is the relationship between frequency deviation and message frequency?

**Answer**

The **frequency deviation is primarily determined by the message amplitude**, not the message frequency.

However, the **rate of frequency variation follows the frequency of the message signal**.

---

# Part C – Spectral Composition of FM Signals

FM signals contain **many frequency components**, known as **sidebands**.

These sidebands appear at:

f = fc ± nfm

where:

fc = carrier frequency  
fm = message frequency  
n = integer value (1, 2, 3, ...)

Unlike AM, which has only two sidebands, FM produces **multiple sidebands**, making its spectrum more complex.

# Observations

Key observations from the experiment:

- FM signals maintain **constant amplitude**
- Carrier **frequency varies with the message**
- Speech signals produce **continuous frequency variation**
- FM signals contain **many spectral components**

# Conclusion

This experiment demonstrated the fundamental principle of **frequency modulation** using the Emona Telecoms-Trainer 101.

The experiment confirmed that:

- FM encodes information in **frequency variations**
- Carrier amplitude remains **constant**
- Message amplitude affects **frequency deviation**
- FM signals contain **multiple spectral components**

These properties make FM more **robust against noise**, which is why it is commonly used in **radio broadcasting and communication systems**.
